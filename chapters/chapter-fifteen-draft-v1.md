# Chapter Fifteen — Bearing

I wake up and something is missing and it takes me until the kettle to find out what.

It is not a sound. The radiator is doing its thing and the alley is doing its thing and a truck is backing up somewhere with the beep going, and none of that is it. It is not the light either, though the light is doing something I will get to. I stand at the counter with my hand on the kettle waiting for it to be hot enough to be worth it, and I take a kind of inventory, the one I do in the morning without calling it one, and every drawer is where I left it, and then I get to the top drawer, the one behind the eyes, and it is empty.

There is a place behind my eyes where a pressure has lived since roughly the first week of October. Not pain, most days. A pressure. A thumb on a bruise that is not quite a bruise. I stopped noticing it the way Maddie says she stopped noticing her retainer, which is to say I noticed it constantly and filed it constantly, and this morning it is not there, and the not-there is so loud I stand at the counter and hold still for it.

The light through the blind is the flat white of a Christmas Eve that has decided not to snow. It is on the counter. It is on the back of my hand. It is not getting into my eyes.

I make the coffee. I drink it in the kitchen with the blind up, which I have not done since the fall, and I wait for the pressure to come back the way a thing comes back when you have gotten cocky about it, and it does not.

---

The flight was Monday. Or the flight was going to be Monday and then the airline sent the text that starts *we're sorry* and ends with a link, and the link had the twenty-sixth in it, and I said fine to the twenty-sixth. I said it the way I say yes to Brendan. My mother took it well, which is to say she took it in the voice she has been using since October, the good-impression voice, and said Christmas was a day like any other and they would have the ham whenever I got there, and I believed about a third of that and let her have the rest.

So it is Christmas Eve and I am in Avondale and the bag is out. It is on the bed, half-packed, in the state a bag gets into when the trip it was for gets moved: the sweaters in, the toothbrush not, a book on top that I have no intention of reading. It is the same bag I took to Denver and it still has the tag on it from Denver, which is the kind of thing I could remove in four seconds and have been not-removing for two years.

Cara said take the days. She said it on Friday without looking up, the way I knew she would, and so today is not a work day and I have the laptop open on the kitchen table anyway, because a laptop open on a kitchen table is what a day off looks like when you live alone.

At 9:40 my cousin texts.

*u hear about this. some guy died at hanlon's last night. dad just called from wheeling, he knew him a little. bar fight. on christmas week. god.*

I read it twice. I read it a third time for the one word in it that is doing something, and the word is not *died* and it is not *wheeling*.

It is *hanlon's*.

I know that word. I know it the way I would know a song I had never listened to on purpose. Somebody said it on the Blue Line in the spring, a man on the phone, *no, at hanlon's, hanlon's,* twice, for somebody on the other end who had never been where he had been. The man at the bookstore said it, or a name near enough to it, the afternoon with the snow, about a bar his uncle had drunk himself out of in some river town, and I filed it under his uncle. Someone at the yoga studio. I could not tell you who or when or what about, only that the word has a shape and the shape is not new to me, and I have been hearing it in this city, on and off, for most of a year, from people who had no business saying it to me.

I look at the text. Nora, I think, for the first time all year, which is my cousin's name, and which I have apparently been keeping in a drawer with the rest.

I type back *oh no* and delete it. I type *that's awful, is your dad ok* and delete it. I type nothing, and I open a tab, and I look.

---

It is four sentences on the site of the Wheeling paper, above the fold of the page in the spot where the paper puts the thing that happened last night.

A man died Tuesday evening following an altercation at a bar on the city's north side. Police were called to Hanlon's at approximately 9:15 p.m. A twenty-two-year-old Wheeling man is in custody. The deceased has been identified as Dale Whitlatch, 46, of Wheeling.

That is the whole of it. There is a picture, the kind the paper takes off a profile page because there is nothing else: a man in his forties in a ball cap, squinting at the sun, with a dog against his chest. A big dog, a shepherd of some kind, with a gray muzzle. He is holding her like she is smaller than she is, which she does not seem to know either. He is not smiling exactly. He is a man with his dog.

I do not know him. I want to be clear about that, to myself, in this chair. I have never seen this man. I did not know his name until nine forty-four this morning. He is not Lauren, whom I met once at the end of a table and who texted me about maps. He is a stranger in a town I left when I was six, in a bar I have never been in, on a night I was here, in Avondale, asleep, with a pressure behind my eyes that is now, this morning, gone.

And I am sitting in my kitchen with my hand flat on the table, and I am not thinking anything about him at all. I am thinking about the bar.

---

Here is what I do for a living. Somebody gives me a spreadsheet. The spreadsheet has things in it that happened in places: deliveries, stops, addresses, a truck that was late forty times. It is a list. Lists lie by being lists; they put things one under another as if that were the relationship between them. My job is to take the list and put it on the ground, on a map, at the actual place where each thing happened, and then to stand back. Usually it is nothing. Usually it is a truck that is late forty times because its driver takes the same bad left. But sometimes the dots, put where they actually go, make a shape the list was hiding, and the shape has an edge, and the edge points somewhere.

I have a folder. I have never called it that until now. It is not a folder. It is the place I have been putting things all year that I did not want to look at, and I have been putting them there with a joke on top like a paperweight, and this morning, with no pressure behind my eyes and a stranger's dog on my screen, I take the joke off.

I do what I would do for a client.

The coffee shop in Logan Square where Lauren and I were going to meet and did not. The stop I get off at early, three or four times a week now, the Western one, for no reason I have ever given myself. The garage on Rockwell where the car has been sitting since Thanksgiving at a hundred and eighty a month. The backyard in Andersonville where I met her, the end of the table where the people who find me find me. The building on twenty-four, north windows. The high table on Milwaukee. The train tunnel past Western where it sat for eleven minutes.

That is Chicago. I put them down one at a time, plain pins, the ugly default kind, and I do not look at the shape yet because I am not done.

Then the others. Not addresses. I do not need addresses for these. Minneapolis, the apartment on the lake where the neighbor knocked with a casserole for a reason she could not explain. Houston, the school. Denver. Portland for eleven months. Madison. Phoenix, which I keep forgetting I lived in and which does not forget me. Seven cities in ten years, and I put the pin in each one at the center, because the center is close enough at this scale and the scale is the point.

And then I put the last two in.

I know Maggie's address. It is on twenty-some envelopes in a shoebox on my closet floor and it has been on them since I was seven, and I put a pin at it, up on the hill in a town I cannot picture, and then I look up the bar, because I have never known where the bar is, and the map finds it in one try.

It is four blocks from her.

I sit back.

I know what eleven points prove. Eleven points prove nothing. Lauren said it in a backyard and I said it to her first, and she thought it was the smartest thing she had heard that month: maps lie. A map will make a pattern out of anything if you want one; it will draw a road between two dots because that is what a map is for. Seven cities on the map of a country with a lot of cities in it, plus a bar, plus a dead woman's mailing address, plus the places where a woman who takes the train got off the train, is not data. It is a woman on Christmas Eve with a laptop. I could not show this to Cara. I could not show it to anyone and keep my job.

And they have a bearing.

They do. I have looked at ten thousand layers and I know what a nothing looks like and I know what a something looks like and this is not a nothing. Chicago is the last of them. Every one of them, put where it actually went, sits on the near side of something, and the something is southeast, and it does not get less true when I zoom out, and at a certain zoom the whole ten years is one loose line with a bend in it, like a river, going one way.

I said, on my birthday, coming up from the garage, that it was not anything. I have said that so many times this year that it stopped being a sentence and became a place I could stand.

The place is not there anymore.

And the thing that comes up in me when it goes is not what I would have guessed. I would have guessed fear, and there is fear, there is a lot of it, but it is under something. What is on top is the feeling I get on the twenty-fourth floor at the north windows on the days the haze is gone, when you can see all the way up the lake to where it stops being a lake and starts being sky and there is no line between them: the feeling of the thing being much bigger than the window, and of the window being, somehow, for me. It is nearly a good feeling. It is a terrible one. It is both of those at the same time and I do not have a drawer for it, and I sit at my own kitchen table on Christmas Eve with the map open and my hands in my lap and I let it be the size that it is.

Then I think about the pins again. The people at the end of tables. The neighbor with the casserole. Lauren, who texted me a week later out of nowhere, still thinking about the thing I said. A man I never met who ended up, night after night for a week, my cousin's father says, at a bar four blocks from the one address I have in that town.

I have been bringing people.

Not on purpose. Not one time on purpose. But I have been the near side of something for as long as I have been anything, and people who are looking for the near side of something have been finding it, and I have handled every one of them and gone home, and one of them is in a paper this morning with his dog.

I do not cry. I want that noted. I sit with it and it is very large and it does not make me cry, and after a while, because I am who I am, I zoom in on the bar and look at the street view, and it is a flat brick corner with a beer sign in the window, and I laugh once, out loud, in the kitchen, at how ordinary it is.

---

My mother forwards me a photograph at 12:20.

No message. No *look at this* or *thought you'd want to see*, which is how I know it cost her something to send, because my mother annotates everything. It is a photograph of a photograph: a print lying on a tabletop I do not recognize, a wood grain, a corner of something that might be a box, somebody's kitchen light on it from above. Whoever is going through the apartment has been sending her these. The things she might want. She told me on the twenty-fourth floor she could not think of one thing.

The print is black and white.

A woman is asleep in a hospital bed with a baby on her chest. The woman is my mother at thirty-three and I have never seen her look like that, wrung out and finished and so far under that her mouth is open a little, one hand on the baby's back, not holding, resting, because the holding is done. The light is from the side, a window out of frame, and it lies along both of them the way light lies on something that has been set down carefully. The baby is a day old. I know it is a day old because I know when I was born and I know what the light in Wheeling looks like at the end of a December afternoon, apparently, though I could not have told you that ten seconds ago.

The baby's eyes are open.

She is looking at the camera. At whoever was holding it. Not the vague blue nowhere-look that babies have in the pictures on people's fridges. She is looking straight up the lens at the person on the other end of it, with her whole face, like somebody has just said her name in a room where she did not think anyone knew it.

I know who was holding the camera.

And I am not ready for what it does. I have been waiting all morning, honestly, for the pressure to come back, for the thumb to find the bruise, and instead something goes the other direction, down, out of my head and into my chest, and it is not a weight. It is warm. It sits behind the bone in the middle of me and opens there like a hand opening, and I take a breath I did not plan to take, a big one, all the way down, the first one in months, and my eyes are wet before I have any idea why.

I am not sad. I am sitting in my kitchen crying and I am not sad. It is the other thing. It is the north-window thing, the too-big thing, but it has come down out of my eyes and gotten warm on the way and turned into something I have no name for except that I am thirty years old and someone has been looking at me like that since I was one day old and I did not know. Someone knew. Someone stood at the end of a hospital bed in 1995 with a camera and knew what she was looking at, and kept it, and is dead, and it came to me on Christmas Eve on a stranger's kitchen table by way of my mother, who could not think of one thing she wanted.

I put the phone face down. I put my hand flat on my own chest, on the sternum, where it is, and I sit like that. The warm does not go. The pressure behind my eyes does not come back. I understand, without being able to say how, that it is not going to; that whatever was living up there has moved, and this is where it lives now, and this is what it is like.

It is like being seen. That is all. It is like being seen and it is the size of the lake.

---

Maddie calls at one.

Not texts. Calls. Maddie has called me on the phone maybe four times since college, and the last one was when her father was in the hospital, and I look at her name on the screen with my face still wet and I know, before I pick up, that it is going to hold.

"You're crying," she says. That is her hello.

"I'm not—" and then I am laughing, which sounds the same on the phone, apparently, because she waits. "I'm fine. I'm actually fine. It's a long day."

"It's one."

"It's a long one."

She is in Seattle. It is eleven there and it is raining, which she reports like a colleague's promotion, with a sense that the rain has not earned it. She has been up since six because Steve, of the asteroid, is hosting and Steve believes in a *timeline*. She tells me about the timeline. She does the voice. I put the phone on speaker and put it on the table next to the closed laptop and I lie down on the kitchen floor, which is a thing I do about once a year, and I laugh at Maddie's Steve on the floor of my kitchen until my stomach hurts.

Somewhere in the middle I tell her. Not all of it. I could not do all of it to Maddie, who has been right about me for twelve years and has never once needed the reasons. I tell her a man died in a bar in the town I was born in and I do not know him. I tell her my mother sent me a picture of myself at one day old. I tell her I am not going to Minneapolis on the twenty-sixth.

There is a pause on the line. It is a real pause; the line does not do anything. It is the first pause I have been allowed all year that did not end with the call ending.

"You're going to drive," Maddie says.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't say a lot of things. Hannah. Han. I have known you since the second week. You have been not-driving somewhere since, what. Halloween."

"October."

"Since October. And I have been letting you, because I am a very good friend and also I was on a timeline." I hear her move, a door, the rain getting louder and then shutting off. "Do you want me to tell you it's a bad idea? I can. I have the whole thing ready. Christmas Eve, five hundred miles, a bar fight, your mother."

"Do you think it's a bad idea."

"I think you're going to do it. I've never seen you do anything you weren't already doing. I'd just like to be on record as the person you told first."

"You are."

"Good. Then go. Call me from Indiana. Indiana needs the company."

We stay on eleven more minutes after that, about nothing, about the ranunculus and whether they are dead yet, about a thing at her office, and then she says she has to go check on a ham that Steve is afraid of, and she says *Merry Christmas, dummy,* and I say it back, and it is fifty-three minutes, and it was never once going to drop, and I lie on my kitchen floor for a minute after with the phone on my chest and understand that I have been living for a year in a house where the phones did not work, and that this one, this one number, always did.

---

I play the recording before I go.

Not on the headphones. On the speaker, on the counter, with the blind up and my coat on, forty-one minutes of my mother and Maggie on the phone about Thanksgiving. I have listened to it a dozen times in a dark bedroom with the phone against my ear like a secret. I have never let it out into a room. And I let it out, and it fills the kitchen, Maggie bossing my mother about the relish, the two of them laughing at something from 1985 that neither of them will explain, and I pack the toothbrush and take the Denver tag off the bag, finally, four seconds, and put the bag by the door, and it plays.

When it stops the kitchen is not empty. I do not know how else to say it. It is the way a room is when somebody has gone into the next one. I stand in it a second. Then I get the key.

It is where I left it. In the coat, where it has been riding since my birthday, and I do not take it out of the pocket so much as close my hand on it and go.

---

The car starts on the first turn.

It has been sitting on the second level of the garage on Rockwell since Thanksgiving and it starts like I drove it yesterday, and I sit with my hands on the wheel and the engine going and I think, this is going to be the day everything works, and I do not know yet whether that is good news.

The Kennedy is empty. It is Christmas Eve at two in the afternoon and the city has done its driving. I go down through the Loop with the lake on my left, gray, all the way to where it stops being a lake, and the skyway, and the steel country south of the city where everything is either on fire or has been, and then Indiana opens up flat and brown with the sky on it, and I call my mother from a rest stop past Gary because there are some things you do not do at seventy miles an hour.

She answers on the first ring.

"Hi, honey." It is her voice. Not the impression. Hers, on Christmas Eve, with something in the oven behind it. "Are you at the airport? I thought it was the twenty-sixth."

"I'm in Indiana."

She does not say anything for a second. I can hear the kitchen. I can hear my father in the garage, which is a thing you can hear.

"I'm driving to Wheeling," I say. "I'll be there tonight. I don't know where I'm staying. I don't—Mom, a man died at Hanlon's. Nora's dad called her. I know that's not a reason. I don't have a reason. I'm just going."

I wait for her to tell me it is a bad idea. She would be right. It is five hundred miles on Christmas Eve toward a town I cannot remember, for a reason I cannot say, on the strength of a map I could not show anyone and a photograph of myself at a day old that she sent me without a word.

"Call your uncle when you get in," my mother says. "He has the room over the garage. It's got a space heater. Tell him I said."

That is all she says. That is all my mother says, and I stand in a rest stop in Indiana with the wind coming across the parking lot and understand that she is not surprised. That she has not been surprised for a long time. That whatever I am going to find in Wheeling, my mother has known where it was since before I could hold my own head up, and has been standing between me and it for thirty years, and has just, on Christmas Eve, on the phone, stepped aside.

"I love you," I say.

"Drive careful. Text me from the hotel. From the room. Text me."

I get back in the car.

The map says five hours and nine minutes. It said five thirty-nine on Thursday. I do not look at that for long. Indianapolis, then 70, then the bottom of Ohio, then the narrow part where the state comes up between the other states like it snuck in. The sun is already going, off to my right, and the road is dry and the radio finds a station and keeps it, and somewhere past the Illinois line, which I did not notice crossing, I roll the window down an inch for the cold and put my hand up into it, and the wind takes my fingers and I let it, and I have not once, since Rockwell, thought about the way back.
