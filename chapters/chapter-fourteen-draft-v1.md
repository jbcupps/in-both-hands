# Chapter Fourteen — The Tap

The first thing Mike Bednarski taught anybody was how to quit.

Before a grip, before a stance, before which end of the belt went where. He sat the new ones down in a row on the mat and showed them. Two fingers, twice, on the other guy, on the floor, on yourself if that was all you could reach. Say it out loud if your hands were tied up. Stomp if your mouth was too. It meant stop and it meant now, and the other half of it, the half Mike said slower, was that when you felt it on you, you let go. Not in a second. Not when you were sure. You let go like dropping a rod that had gone live in your hand.

Cole had tapped something like ten thousand times in two and a half years. To brown belts, to a hundred-and-thirty-pound librarian, once to a sixteen-year-old whose mother was watching from the folding chairs. It had stopped costing him anything the first month. And he had been tapped, which was the side of it he liked better if he was honest: that small double pat on the forearm, polite as a knock, from a man whose neck he had. That was the whole sport. Everything else was how you got there. He could go as hard as he had in him, which he could not do anywhere else on earth, because there was a door built into it and both people knew where the door was.

It was a Tuesday. Tuesday was gi night. His bag was behind the seat of the truck with a clean gi folded in it, and he did not drive to Pittsburgh.

* * *

He drove to the river. There was a gravel pull-off north of the bridge where his father had put a borrowed boat in, six summers running, and he sat there with the engine on and the heater ticking and the carnations on the seat beside him in their grocery-store bucket. He had known at three that the cemetery gate locked at dusk. He had bought them anyway.

The river usually took something off him. He had never looked at why. Tonight it was black water, and the lights on the far bank came across it in long shaking lines and stopped short of him.

He had tried the things. He would go over the list later, when he had time to go over lists, and it was not a short one. The funeral: he had put on the tie and driven, and come to in the shop lot with the tie in his pocket. The cemetery: four runs at it and one arrival, in November, with gas-station flowers because the florist by the gate had closed. Her street, twice, and both times he had ended up behind Hanlon's instead with the engine running and no memory of choosing. The mat, where he went so hard for three weeks that Mike took him aside and asked, carefully, if everything was all right at home. The heaviest work in the shop. The hill behind his mother's house, at a run, in the dark. Not drinking. Drinking. None of it was a bad idea. None of it had anywhere to put the thing down.

The back way cost him six minutes and he had been paying them for two years: over the creek on the low bridge, up through the blocks where the houses quit and the warehouses started, then north along the water. At twenty it had been so his mother's friends would not clock the truck. He was not sure what it was for anymore. Habit is a road too.

He parked in back by the dumpster. The alley door had swollen in its frame years ago and there was a knack to it, a lift and a hip. He had the knack.

* * *

Third stool from the alley end. The varnish was gone off the rail there in two long ovals, and he pushed his sleeves up and put his arms down in them because that was where they went.

Hanlon set a beer in front of him before he had his jacket settled. Hanlon was a big man gone soft, with a face that did not do much, and for most of two years he had treated Cole like the furniture, which Cole had taken as a compliment. Since October he stood differently. He would start back up the bar and stop short of where he was going, and keep Cole in the corner of his eye from there, like a man working on the ground who knows what is hanging over him.

"You eat?"

"Yeah."

He hadn't. Hanlon looked at him one second longer and went up the bar, and did not go all the way.

It was two days before Christmas and the place looked it, barely. Somebody's wife had run a strand of colored lights along the top of the back-bar mirror sometime in the nineties and nobody had taken it down since, and about half of it still worked. The television in the corner had a bowl game on with the sound off, two schools nobody in the room had gone to or could have found. Six or seven men. The one in the booth by the window who came in at eight and left at close, whose wife had been gone ten years and who had never once mentioned her. Two guys from the glass plant splitting a pitcher. Davy at the front corner, home between ships, sixty years old and loud like a man who has spent his life talking over engines.

Davy was telling the one about the chief engineer in Mobile who went ashore to buy his wife a birthday card and came back four days later with the card, unsigned, and another woman's name tattooed on his neck. He did the voices. He did the chief trying to explain the neck to the captain. Cole had heard that story take the whole bar apart. He had seen Hanlon laugh at it, which was a thing you could tell your grandchildren.

Tonight Davy got to the card and the room let it fall. One of the glass plant guys breathed out through his nose. Davy looked around like a man who has thrown a line to a dock and watched it go in the water.

There is a thing that happens with a bad ground. The machine is on, the dial is right, the rod is right. You strike and you get an arc, and the arc is wrong. It wanders, it spits, it won't bite the plate. And a man can stand there ten minutes adjusting everything in front of him before he thinks to walk back and look at the clamp. The room was like that. It had been like that Sunday and it had been like that Monday. Everything in front of him was right. Somewhere behind it, something had come off of whatever it used to be fastened to.

He drank slowly. He did most things slowly in a room with other people in it. It was not his temperament. It was a practice, ten years old, and nobody had ever caught him at it.

The gather was in him from the elbows down. He knew it like he knew a tank was near empty by the heft, without looking. A bolt taken a quarter turn past spec, and held there eight weeks, and the one place he had ever been able to back it off was closed.

Two stools toward the front there was a man in a windbreaker zipped to the throat. Not one of theirs. A week of nights in here and nobody had learned what he drank. He was talking low to the bottles. Cole caught *thirteen years* and let it go by. He was full up.

Hanlon brought the second one without being asked.

* * *

*You're carrying something in your forearms.*

Two Decembers back. She had been on her way to the bathroom and she stopped behind him, close enough that he felt it. He turned around to a small woman of sixty-some in a fleece vest with a hospital lanyard still around her neck, and she was not looking at his face. She was looking at him like the old hands at the shop looked at a bead: not at the man, at the work. He said, *What.* It was the only word he had, and it was a lie, and she let him keep it. *Come sit with me when you're done,* she said, like asking him to pass the salt, and went on. He sat with her. He left at two.

Her apartment had three lamps and she never turned on anything else. One by the door with the keys. One at the end of the couch. One up on the refrigerator, of all places, putting its light down over the stove. He could have walked those rooms blind. He knew which board by the kitchen gave and which cushion was his.

She never told him what it was. She gave him three words and the words were theirs. The gather was what he walked in with. The let-down was what happened on the couch, when she put her hand on his shoulder and he pushed. All of it. What he had not pushed at anything since the week his father died, when he put his fist through his bedroom wall and stood looking at the hole. Her hand did not so much as tighten. It went out of him like a load coming off a chain hoist, the whole rig easing at once. The long quiet was after. An hour, sometimes. He could sit with his full weight on the cushion and his hands open on his knees and not be holding anything off of anybody. There was nothing grand in it. It was a rest. It was the only room he had ever been in where his body did not have to stand guard over everyone else in it.

The first time, he wept for an hour and did not know what he had done. She brought him a glass of water and made nothing of it. After that it was twice a month. *Can I come over and let down.* *Come on.* Fifty times, near enough. When it was over he would carry her cup to the sink. He never cursed in front of her, not once, and could not have said why.

In all that time she never said she was sick.

Hanlon told him. October, a slow night, the big man leaning in on both hands to say one careful sentence, and then finding something that needed doing at the other end of the bar. Cole remembered that he had said thank you. He remembered thinking that was a strange thing to have said.

* * *

Halfway through the third one he got up to piss and did not make it to the bathroom.

The back corridor was cinderblock under a cream paint gone the color of teeth. He stopped halfway down it and put his forehead on the block, and his palms flat on either side of his head, and breathed the long way, down into the belt.

*Fuck,* he said, to the wall, quietly, and heard it like he sometimes did, in his babka's kitchen, in her voice. He let that go by.

Then he pushed. He did not mean to. There was nobody there to be careful of, and it came up through him and went into eight inches of block, and the block did what block does with it. Nothing came back. Nothing eased. He stood there two minutes with his eyes shut, a man trying to pour something into a wall.

Out in the room a chair went over.

* * *

He would be asked a good many times, by people with forms, what it was about. He never had a better answer than the true one. Davy had started the story again from the top, louder, as if the room just hadn't heard him. The man from the window booth had come up to settle his tab and said, not even unkindly, that last time it was Galveston. Davy said it was never Galveston. The man said all right. Davy said don't *all right* me. That was all of it. Cole had heard men say those things to each other in a hundred rooms, and the words were never the reason, and in this room it had never once come to anything. This was the room where it didn't.

Hanlon was already out from behind the bar. *Hey. Hey, now,* low, the voice he kept for this. Cole had watched that voice and one big hand settle this room a dozen times without anybody deciding to be settled.

The lights sagged. Every one at once, to a brown he could barely see faces in, and they hung there.

He saw Hanlon get to them and put his arm out between them, and he saw the arm stop. A foot short. The big man stood with his hand in the air between two drunks, touching neither, and looked at it. At his own hand.

Davy swung. It was a sailor's haymaker, wide and slow, thrown from somewhere around last week, and the man from the window booth was not where it was going anymore. The man in the windbreaker was. He had stood up off his stool with his money in his hand, and he was looking at the front door.

There was no deciding in it. Ten years of being the careful one in every room, and the care was exactly what moved him: somebody was about to get hurt who had nothing to do with it. He came out of the corridor mouth low and fast, got his head outside Davy's arm and his own arms locked around the ribs, and turned him.

He has never been able to make the floor plan work. He knows where he was and where Davy was, and he knows that turning a heavy man off a punch takes him away from whatever he was swinging at. That is what the turn is for. But there was a stool, or a foot, or the dark, and all three of them went over in a heap, and when the floor quit moving he was kneeling in spilled beer with Davy's shirt in both hands and Davy was lying across somebody.

He hauled him off. Davy rolled onto his back and lay there with his chest going, done. Done the second he hit. There had never been any fight in it.

The man in the windbreaker sat up.

Cole's hand was already in his collar. When had that happened. The face was a foot from his, the color of putty in that light, saying something that went under the noise of every man in the place coming to his feet. The eyes went over Cole like Cole was part of the floor. Then he shoved, hard, both hands, and twisted, and went.

He was going for the door. It took Cole most of a week to see it, and when he saw it, it was because of the money. A man does not get his cash out to start a fight. He had been trying to leave since before it began.

That night, on his knees in the brown dark with a collar tearing out of his fist, he read it wrong. He read it as a man going at somebody. There is an answer to that, and he had drilled it three nights a week.

He took the back. It was there and he took it. Legs around the waist, one shin locked behind his own other knee. Right arm under the chin. Right hand to his own left biceps, left hand behind the head, elbows in. He did not squeeze so much as close, evenly, like a vise. Mike would have stood over it and found nothing to fix. Cole has had to live with that. He did it right.

The man did not tap.

He had never been on a mat. Nobody had ever sat him down in a row and shown him two fingers, twice. He did not know there was a door. He bucked. He got both hands on Cole's forearm and tore at it. He drove a heel into the floorboards, twice, three times, and none of it was the signal, and Cole's arms had been built for two and a half years around a pair of things, the tap and the letting go, built like a hand gets built around the handle of its own hammer. They held on and waited for the first half.

The man went slack.

Still no tap. His arms did not know what to do with slack by itself. They waited for the rest of it.

One.

Two.

Three.

Then something in him understood, not what, only that it was wrong, and he opened his arms.

He has counted those since more times than he ever tapped. Everything up to them he could put on something. The room. Davy. The dark. The man turning. Those three he could not put anywhere. He had been there for them.

* * *

The lights came back. He did not see them do it. Hanlon could never say afterward whether they had gone all the way out.

On the mat, when somebody goes out, you lay him flat and lift his legs and say his name, and in five seconds he is blinking at the ceiling, embarrassed, asking what happened. Cole had put two men to sleep in his life who were too proud to tap. Both were awake before he finished asking if they were okay.

He laid the man flat. He lifted his legs. He did not have a name to say, so he said, *Hey. Hey, buddy. Hey.*

Five seconds. Ten.

"Call 911," he said, and did not know his own voice. Hanlon was at the wall phone with the cord pulled straight, saying the name of the bar.

He knew what came next. He had watched his mother do it on a kitchen floor on a Tuesday evening when he was fifteen. He had not thought about her hands in ten years, and his own went to the same place. Heel of the palm on the breastbone. Elbows locked. Hard and fast. He counted those too, out loud, to thirty and again, his bare forearms straight over a stranger's chest, the zipper of the windbreaker cold under the edge of his hand.

Davy had gotten himself up onto a stool and was telling nobody, "I never touched him. I never touched him." The booth by the window was empty. Its man had used the dark to go out the front, and not one person could say when, and that would matter later, to the people with forms.

Somebody who did not have the knack fought the alley door and won.

She came as far as the mouth of the corridor and stopped. Seventies. A car coat, her keys still in her fist. Nobody comes into a bar like that for a drink; she had come like a neighbor crossing the street toward smoke. She took in the room, and the man on the floor, and whatever that did to her she folded up and put away for later. Then she looked at Cole's arms.

Not at him. At his forearms, working. At the freckles, and at what was under the freckles. In two years exactly one person had looked at him like that. The hair stood up on his neck.

She took a step in. "Son," she said. "Let me—"

He did not hear help. He heard somebody who could see. He never lifted a hand; his hands were busy. What went at her went like heat off a fresh weld, nothing to look at, and she stopped as if she had laid her palm on it. Her body got the message before her face did. One step back, then another, her free hand flat against her own breastbone, and she gave him a look he has turned over a great many times since, and went back out into the cold. He never learned her name.

Twenty-eight. Twenty-nine. Thirty.

There was a table against the far wall, under the dead clock, that nobody ever used. Somebody was sitting at it. He had not seen anyone come in. Whoever it was was not watching the man on the floor. They were watching him, in no hurry at all, like he and his father used to watch a line that had started to move. He did not turn his head. When he looked again there was a glass on the table and nobody behind it, and he could not have sworn the glass was new.

* * *

They came in the front, the medics first and then two cops and then more. Somebody's hands took his shoulders and moved him off, and he let them. He sat back on his heels with his palms open on his thighs and watched them work. They were good, and fast, and he knew.

He had known on the third count. He had had his arm around it when it went.

"Who was in this?"

"I was in the fight," Cole said.

He said it from the floor, looking up. He did not say the rest. He had not worked out the rest, and he is a man who does not say a thing until he has it. When they told him to stand he stood, and turned around before he was asked, and put his hands behind him. He had seen enough television.

The cop who cuffed him was not much older than he was. He pushed Cole's sleeves down for him first, which was a decent thing to do on a night like that, and he saw the arms.

"Welder?"

"Yes sir."

The cop nodded. He had an uncle.

They took him out the street door, which in two years he had never once used. It was a different bar from that side. There was a beer sign in the window he had only ever seen the back of. Around the corner in the gravel the truck sat locked, with a clean gi behind the seat and a bucket of carnations on it. It was cold enough. They would keep.

A hand on top of his head, and he was folded into the back of the car.

Welder. That was what anyone saw, looking, and it was true. He had the marks for it, three years of them, elbow to wrist. He had the other marks too, on the same skin. In his whole life one person had looked at his arms long enough to tell the two apart. The second had just backed out an alley door with her hand on her chest.
