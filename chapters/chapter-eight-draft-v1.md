**Chapter Eight**

Thursday comes up gray over Avondale and I am at the counter before I am properly awake, the kettle going, the radio on so the kitchen has a voice in it while I do the things a person does before they go downtown, and the things are the kettle and the toast and the first coffee held and not yet tasted, and the radio is at the traffic the way it is every weekday at this hour, the inbound Kennedy, the usual slow stretch by the usual ramp, a man's voice that has told me about this same ramp four hundred mornings and that I have come to find restful in the way of things that ask nothing back.

The text is on my phone when I take it off the charger. The phone says it came at 7:51. I had the phone in my hand at eight to kill the second alarm and I am nearly certain there was nothing on it then, but *nearly certain* is the kind of certainty that does not survive being looked at, so I set down the *when* of it and read the *what*.

*hey. did you hear about lauren. it's so awful. call me whenever.*

It is from my cousin, and I have to hold the name a second to find the drawer I have put it in.

Lauren. There was a Lauren at my cousin's in August, at the backyard thing for somebody's birthday where I knew my cousin and the dog and no one else, and I had done what I do at those, which is drift to the end of the table and end up next to the one other person who is also not quite at the party, and the one other person was Lauren. She had a denim jacket with a butterscotch melted flat into the pocket that she showed me like a small problem we were now in together. She told me inside of four minutes that she had moved back from Portland because her lease and her relationship had ended the same week and she had decided to take it as a sign even though she did not believe in signs. She was easy the way people are easy when they have stopped performing being fine. We talked most of an hour. She asked what I did and I told her about the maps, and she asked the actual second question, the one almost nobody asks, and when I got up to find my cousin she put her number in my phone and said we should get coffee, you are easy to talk to, and I said yes, and I meant the kind of yes you mean and do not act on.

She texted a week after — *cousin gave me your number hope that's ok, still thinking about that thing you said about how maps lie* — and we went back and forth a little, and a coffee got floated for a Saturday, and the Saturday did not happen, and another one got floated and did not happen either, and then the thread did the thing threads do, which is stop, with neither of us having decided to stop it. I have not thought about her since. I could not have told you her last name an hour ago.

I text my cousin back. *oh no — what happened.* The dots come and go long enough that I put the phone face down and pick it up again. *overdose. her sister found her sunday. it's on her sister's page.* Then a second bubble a few seconds behind the first: *wasn't sure if you two ever ended up getting coffee.*

We did not ever end up getting coffee.

I type three things and delete all three and send the heart, because there is nothing I can say that the heart does not say more honestly, and I put the phone in my coat pocket. The coffee has gone the temperature coffee goes when you forget it. I pour it out. I do not make more.

I did not know her. This is the thing I keep arriving back at on the train, my forehead on the cold window and the tunnel going past at Western and the gray coming back at Damen — that I met a woman one time in a backyard and traded eleven texts with her and have no standing, none, to feel the way I am feeling, which is the way you feel when a door has closed in a house you do not live in. People I have known for years have died and I have felt less. There is something nearly embarrassing in it, a grief the wrong size for its occasion, and I tell myself the thing you tell yourself, that it is not really her, it is the season, it is sleeping badly, it is the other thing I am not going to think about on a train. It has nothing to do with me. I met her once.

---

Downtown there is a dataset that wants cleaning before a noon handoff, and I open it and the columns are where I left them Tuesday and I cannot get my attention to sit down inside them. Across the half-wall Dev is telling someone about the weekend at his in-laws' in a way that has a beginning and a middle, and Dev tells a story the way you would want one told, the boring parts left in on purpose, and on an ordinary day I am his audience without his knowing it, but today the words go past me and catch on nothing, the way rain goes past a window it is not going to come through. I lose the dataset and find it again. At eleven my manager stops to ask if the handoff is on track and I tell her it is, and it is not, and I make it true between eleven and noon by a kind of force I am going to feel tomorrow.

At three I tell her I have one of my headaches, which is true — it came on around two and set up behind my eyes the way it does — and she says go home, you look pale, and I go.

---

I take the Blue Line and I do not take it the whole way. I get off at Logan Square, a stop before mine. I could tell you I got off to walk because the headache wanted air and the car was too bright, and that would be true, and it would also be the kind of true I reach for when I have done a thing I cannot account for, because I did not decide to get off at Logan Square. I was at the doors with my hand on the pole and the doors opened and I was on the platform, and the train pulled out, and I stood there doing the small arithmetic of being one stop early, an arithmetic I have done before and have stopped pretending surprises me.

Down the stairs and out onto the boulevard, and it is colder than the train, and the cold is good on my face for about a block, and then the headache turns from the ordinary one into the other kind, the kind with edges, the one that makes the gray afternoon too bright and lays a thin wire of pain along the top of everything I look at. I walk a street I have been down maybe twice. The light is already going, the four-o'clock light, the storefronts coming on.

I stop in front of a coffee shop.

I do not know why I stop. I am simply stopped, on the sidewalk, in front of a place I have never been into, a hand-painted sign in the window and a string of bulbs along the top of the glass, and the bulbs dim down a half-step and come back, dim and come back, the way bulbs do when something is not right in the line. I look at them a moment and then I look through the glass at the people at the small tables with their laptops and their afternoon, and I have the clear, useless feeling that I have come here for a reason and the reason will not give me its name. There is no one in there I know. There is no reason I would know anyone in there. I have never been to this place and have no business in front of it, and I stand in front of it exactly long enough to feel foolish, and then the door opens and two people come out laughing and hold it for me, and I shake my head, no thanks, and I keep walking, west, toward home, and I do not look back at the bulbs.

---

At my building the lamp by the door is the dead lamp it was in November and the month before that. I let myself in. I put the keys in the bowl. I take three ibuprofen at the sink and stand while the cold comes off the window, and I turn off the overhead and leave the lamp on the timer, which is already on, which means it is one of the nights it has decided to be on, and I am grateful to it without looking at the gratitude.

I want to call my mother.

I do not entirely know what I want to say — not about Lauren, who she has never heard of, but something, the way a death at the edge of your week turns you toward the voice you have known the longest. She is at the top of my recents, where she has been since the fall. I press her name.

The call connects and rings once and then it is not a call. The screen counts the seconds — eight, nine — and then it stops counting, and then it tells me the call has ended. The bars are full.

I press her name again.

It rings, or it makes the sound of ringing, and on the fourth ring there is a quarter-second of my mother, the front edge of her *hello*, and then that is gone too, and the screen says the call lasted nineteen seconds. The bars are full the whole time.

I do not call a third time. I tell myself the towers are bad tonight, which is a thing that happens, or a thing I have decided happens, and I send a text instead — *tried to call, line's being weird, talk this weekend? love you* — and I watch it send, and it sends, the text going through the air the call would not. I put the phone face down on the counter. I will call her Saturday from the kitchen with nothing else going on. The wanting is still there, easy, at the top of me, the way it has been since the fall. I just cannot get it down the line tonight.

I make eggs, because eggs are what there is and what I can stand to make, and I put on music while I cook — not the radio, the app, something without words, because words are too much tonight — and I eat standing at the counter the way I have eaten most of my dinners this year.

The music stops in the middle of a song.

Not stops — goes into the gap it goes into, the half-second that is not silence and is not static, and comes back a few seconds later in the middle of a different song, having skipped whatever it skipped. It does it again while I am rinsing the pan. It does it a third time as I sit down with the last of the eggs, the sound dropping out and the kitchen briefly only itself — the refrigerator, the radiator, the street. Each time it comes back. I do not get up to do anything about it. I have stopped getting up to do anything about it.

Maggie's voice is in the phone, the long recording I keep meaning to do something with and do not. I think about playing it. I do not play it. I do not want her voice in the kitchen on top of the rest of it tonight — the dead woman I did know and the dead woman I did not, and the line to my mother that would not hold — and I leave the recording where it is, and that is its own kind of decision, and I let it be one.

---

After the eggs I open the laptop on the kitchen table to do something ordinary with the end of the evening — to look at the sister's page, maybe, the thing my cousin said was there, though I am not sure I want to see it — and I do not go to the sister's page. I open a map. I did not decide to open a map; it is what my hands do when the rest of me has gone quiet, the way other people open a feed or a card game, and I am looking at the middle of the country the way I look at it, the interstates in their gray and the rivers in their blue, and at some point my hands have put a destination in, because there is a route on the screen now, a line drawn from here down through Indiana and along the bottom of Ohio to where the panhandle of West Virginia comes up between the states like an afterthought, to Wheeling, and the box in the corner says five hours and fifty-one minutes, and I do not remember typing it.

I look at it a while. It is a good route. It is the route I would take. The line goes where the land makes it go, down and east and then along the river at the end, and I sit with my finger not quite on the screen, the headache behind my eyes and the music dropping out again somewhere behind me, and I think, the way you think a thing without it being a plan, *I could be there by dark.*

Then I close the laptop. I do not look up a motel. I do not pack a bag. I rinse the plate, and I turn off the lamp the timer would have turned off in an hour anyway, and I lie down on the bed in my clothes in the dark, and the apartment is quiet except for the small sounds a building makes at night, and a long way south and east of here there is a town I was born in and do not remember, and I do not think about it, and after a while I sleep.
