# Chapter Thirteen — The Twenty-Third

The carrier's name was Len and he had carried the hill route for nineteen years.

He knew it by the order of the boxes and not the names, by the dogs, by which porches had a step that gave. The brick four-unit halfway up the street had a bank of four boxes inside the side door, and for eleven years the second box from the left had been the one with the good handwriting on its outgoing, cards mostly, a few a month, addressed in a hand that made a capital *R* with a flourish nobody made anymore. He had noticed the hand before he had ever noticed the woman. The woman he had noticed later. She was home at odd hours. A nurse, he had decided, from the shoes and the times. She said good morning to a mailman as though the mailman were a person.

In October she had been on the side step when he came up, in a robe with a coat over it, and she had handed him an envelope instead of leaving it in the box. He remembered it because it was the only time she had. She had said *would you take this*, and he had said he would, and she had said *thank you, Len*, which he had not known she knew. The envelope was ordinary. It had the flourished *R* on it and a Pittsburgh address, a street in Highland Park, and a name he did not read because he did not read them. He had put it in the outgoing bag on his hip and carried it down the hill and it had gone into the system the way everything went into the system, and he had not thought about it since, and there had been no reason to.

Three weeks later the box had a card in it from the landlord about the tenant having passed, and he had stood in the vestibule a minute holding the day's mail for a woman who was not going to open it, and then he had put it in the box, because that was what you did.

Since then the box had been filling. He put it in anyway.

What he did not know, and could not have, was where the envelope was. It was two cities away, in a tray, in a processing facility off a highway, at the bottom of a stack that had been sorted wrong on a Sunday in October when the sorter's second reader went down for forty minutes and the trays from that window were run through again by hand. The hand had been tired. The tray had gone to a shelf where trays went when their destination read was ambiguous, and the shelf was cleared every two weeks, and on the two Mondays since, the person clearing it had been called away — once for a jam on line four, once for a phone call from a daughter — before reaching the third shelf from the bottom. The envelope was under a catalog for a company that sold tractor parts. Nothing was wrong with it. It was not moving.

Len went on up the hill. At the top the street bent and the sky opened over the river, and there was a hawk on the light pole that had been on the light pole every morning this month, and it did not move when he passed under it, and he had stopped looking up.

* * *

The nurse's name was Cheryl and she was on her fourth week of being late.

Not late to work. She had never been late to work in twenty-two years. Late in the other way — a half-step behind the day, arriving at each thing a beat after the thing had already needed her. She had driven to the wrong patient on a Thursday in November. She had gone to the pharmacy with a list and come home with the list. She had stood in her own kitchen in the dark for a full minute one night in December before she understood that the light was not off, she had simply not turned it on.

She had told her supervisor it was the season. Her supervisor had said the season was hard on everyone and asked was she sleeping. She was sleeping. She was sleeping fine. She woke up. She drank the coffee. She got in the car, and something in the car was not quite where she had put it, and she drove to the house on the north end where Mr. Kovach was dying in the front room his wife had turned into a bedroom in September.

He was eighty-one. He had been a crane operator at the mill and then, after the mill, a crane operator at the port, and he had a photograph of himself in the cab of a crane in 1971 that his wife had put where he could see it. He was past talking most days. He was not past pain. The pump on the pole beside the bed did what the pump did, and the number on the pump was the number Cheryl had set on Monday, and the number had been right on Monday.

At noon on Tuesday the doctor had changed the order. The fax had come to the office. The office had called Cheryl's cell and the call had gone to voicemail, though the phone had been on the seat beside her with full bars, and the voicemail had come through at two-fifteen as a notification she saw and meant to open and did not open, because at two-fifteen she was in the Kovach driveway and Mrs. Kovach was at the storm door with her arms folded, waiting the way the wives wait, and Cheryl went in.

She checked him. She turned him. She looked at the pump and the number on the pump was the number she had set and it was the number she expected to see, and she wrote it on the sheet and initialed the sheet. She sat with Mrs. Kovach in the kitchen and drank half a cup of coffee and said the things she said. Mrs. Kovach asked was it going to be soon and Cheryl said it might be, and that he was comfortable, and that they would keep him comfortable, and she believed it while she said it.

She had said the same things to Maggie Hennessy's cousin in a different kitchen in October. She had stood in that apartment with two other people and a woman she had worked beside for fourteen years and watched the woman go, faster than any of them had planned for, and she had done what there was to do after, and gone home, and been fine, and been late ever since. She did not connect the two. There was no reason to.

In the car the notification was still on the screen. She backed out of the driveway. The radio was on the station it was always on and the station was playing something she liked, and then it was playing the sound a station makes between towns, a long gray hiss with a voice under it, and she reached over and turned it off and forgot there had been anything to open.

* * *

The truck was black, a Ford with a rig in the bed and a West Virginia plate, and it had been parked with the engine running outside a florist's on Market Street for eleven minutes.

The young man in it had meant to go to the cemetery. He had left the shop at three with the foreman's okay and a bucket of carnations already on the seat, because he had learned in November that the florist by the cemetery closed early and he was not going to be caught by that again, and he had driven back across the creek toward the hill with the carnations on the seat and had ended up on Market Street instead, in front of a different florist, with no memory of the turn that had put him there.

He sat with his forearms on the wheel. His phone was on the dash. It had been buzzing since three-ten, and at three-twenty he picked it up and it was the shop, and it was the foreman, and the foreman was sorry, but the Pruitt order — the handrail, the one that was due Friday, the one that had been due last Friday — the customer was standing in the office and was not leaving, and could he come back and burn it out before five, and he would owe him.

He said he would come back.

He drove back across the creek with the carnations on the seat. He parked in the lot behind the shop and did not go in. He sat with the engine off and the cab going cold and his hands on the wheel and looked at the cinderblock wall of the shop, which had a stain on it in the shape of nothing in particular that he had looked at every day for three years, and he sat there for ten minutes by the clock on the dash. He did not weep. He sat with the thing in his forearms that had been in his forearms since October and had nowhere to go, and when the ten minutes were up he got out and went in.

He worked the job. He was good at the job. The handrail was a plain thing, forty feet of it in sections, and he laid the beads down the way his father had shown him to lay a bead, and the shop was loud and warm and there was no one in it who wanted anything from him but the rail. The foreman looked in twice and said nothing. At ten to five the customer took the sections out on a trailer and the foreman said *go on home*, and he said yeah.

It was dark by then. The carnations were on the seat where he had left them, and the cab was cold enough that they would keep, and he sat looking at them a minute before he started the truck. The cemetery gate locked at dusk. He knew that. He had known it at three.

He pulled out of the lot with the heater going and turned toward the creek and did not turn toward home.

* * *

Two blocks north of a bar on a corner, a woman in her seventies stood in a parking lot with her keys in her hand, looking at a light on a pole.

The lot was behind a narrow brick building she had owned since 1988 and practiced out of until 2019, and now leased to a dentist, and the light on the pole had been out for three weeks and the electrician had been out twice and could find nothing wrong with it. It came on when he was standing under it. It went out when he left. She had come by at five to see it for herself, because she was a woman who liked to see a thing for herself before she paid a third bill for it, and she was standing under the pole in the last of the daylight, and the light was out.

She did not think about the light for very long. She was thinking about something else, or was about to, when she stopped.

It was two beats. She would not have been able to say what it was. The air in the lot was wrong in a way that had a direction to it, a draft's direction, and the direction was south, and she stood with her keys in her hand and her face turned a little toward the corner, toward a sound she had not yet decided was a sound.

She had known Maggie Hennessy for thirty years. She had sat with her twice in the back booth of the place on the corner, in the old days, and had understood without either of them saying it that they were two of a kind and would not be making a thing of it. She had not been in the place since Maggie died. She had not wanted to feel what the room was doing without her.

She felt it now, from two blocks off, and she did not have a word for it, and she was old enough to know that not having a word for a thing was not the same as the thing not being there.

She set it down. That was the discipline, and she had practiced it for forty years, and it did not fail her. She turned her face back toward the pole. The light was still out.

She called the electrician from the car. He was sorry; he was on a furnace in Elm Grove; he could come by after his last call, nine, nine-thirty, if she wanted to meet him, and she said she would meet him, because she was going to see this fixed. She sat a moment with the engine running and the lot going dark around her and the one dead pole in it. Then she backed out, and turned north, away from the corner, and drove home to eat something, and she was not, in any sentence she would have said aloud, thinking about anything at all.

She would be back at nine.

* * *

In St. Louis Park, Minnesota, eight hundred miles from the river, a woman named Marilyn stood at her kitchen window with the phone in her hand and looked across two driveways at the Reillys' house.

The kitchen light was on over there. It had been on at odd hours since October. She had seen Beth Reilly come out to the mailbox in November in her house shoes with no coat and stand there a long time with the mail in her hand, and she had meant to go over, and she had not gone over. She had meant to bring something. She had made a pan of bars for it and eaten the bars.

She had known Beth twelve years, since the Reillys moved in. She knew that Beth's friend had died, the one from West Virginia, because Beth had told her at the mailbox in a voice like a person reading an address. She knew that Beth had been *not herself* since, which was the phrase Marilyn used inside her own head for a thing she had seen and did not know what to do about. She had the number in the phone. Her thumb was on it.

She would say *I've been thinking about you*. She would say *I'm just across the way*. She would not say *you're not yourself*, because you did not say that to a woman; you brought a pan of something and let the pan say it.

The kitchen light across the way went off.

Marilyn stood a while longer with the phone in her hand and the dark house across the drives. Then she thought that it was late. It was two days before Christmas. The woman had a daughter and a husband and did not need the neighbor. She would go over tomorrow with the bars. She put the phone face down on the counter and turned off her own light, and she did not go over tomorrow either, and could not, afterward, have said why.

* * *

At the Kovach house on the north end the pump did what the pump did, at the number it had been set to on Monday.

The change in the order was in a fax tray at the office and in a voicemail on a phone on a nightstand, and both would be found in the morning. In the front room that had been a living room the crane operator came up out of the place he had been all week, sometime after eleven, and found that his body was a body again. He was not able to say so. His wife was asleep in the chair with the blanket over her knees the way she slept, and he did not want to wake her, and then he did want to, and could not, and the night went the way that kind of night goes, in a house on a dark street, with the pump doing what it did.

The wife woke at four and knew from the room. She called the number. Cheryl answered on the first ring and was in the car in six minutes, and drove the eleven minutes to the north end in nine, and she did what could be done then, which was not nothing but was not what could have been done at noon, and she did it well, and she held the old woman in the kitchen after, and she said the things you say.

She did not look at the phone until she was back in her own driveway with the sun coming up gray over the hill. The notification was still there. She opened it. She listened to it twice.

She sat in the car a long time with the engine off. On the dash the clock said 7:11. She watched it. It said 7:12. Then she went in.
