# The Road of the Living Water
*Fairy tale · Slavic folklore · Tale the Fourth · Revised August 2026*

The old woman came back a third time, and Vasilisa knew before she opened the door that it would be for bread again, and opened it anyway.

She was the one whose roof had fallen farther along the road, and she had the manner of someone who had been proud for a long time and had lately stopped being able to afford it. Vasilisa sat her by the stove and put the last of the good bread in her hands and did not watch her eat it.

"There's a grove," the woman said, when she could. "Four days east, where the ground goes soft. White birch. There's a spring under it that still comes up clear."

"All the springs come up clear now," Vasilisa said. "It's the ones that come up wrong you have to watch."

"Not like this one." The old woman set the crust down. "The iron birds go round it. Round, mind. Not over. My man's brother drank there when the burning was at its worst and he walked out of that country on legs that had no business carrying him." She looked at the bench where Vasilisa's mother slept, and then at Vasilisa, and did not pretend she hadn't. "It eases the body. It stretches out what's left."

"And?"

"And it doesn't give back what's already been reckoned. Nobody gets that."

Against Vasilisa's side, the doll warmed — a small clean warmth, the warmth of a true thing spoken.

It was the first warmth she had felt from it since the night the High Place burned. Underneath it, the deeper cold went on, the way a stone stays cold under a hand laid on it.

She knew then that the road was hers to walk.

---

She prepared without speaking the whole weight of it. She left the house as well tended as she could and the lampada burning. She asked the neighbour with the good eyes to look in twice a day, and the neighbour, who had her own dead to think about, said yes without asking why. She packed a little bread, clean water for the doll, and the old songs in her memory.

At the last her mother caught her wrist. The grip was still stronger than it looked.

"Four days east," her mother said.

"Yes."

"Take the mended cloak, not the good one. If it tears you'll be glad it's the one you know how to fix."

That was all. Vasilisa crossed herself before the icon, felt her father's early lessons settle down into her own hands where they had always lived, and stepped out into the changed land.

---

The road through the country the Black Host had crossed was long and careful. Scorched earth. Empty places with a stove standing alone in them, which is what a house leaves behind. She walked in the mornings and the late afternoons and lay still under the birch shade in the hours when the sky was busy.

On the second day the iron birds came low.

She heard them before she saw them and she went down into a ditch with her face in the wet leaves, and the sound went over her — not a sound so much as a pressure, something that got in under the ribs and rearranged things. When it had passed she found that she had bitten through her lip and that her hands were flat and open on the mud, and that she had been singing under her breath the whole time without knowing it. One of her mother's. The one about the river that remembers every crossing.

She lay there and finished the verse properly, because a thing half-sung sits in the throat. Then she got up and went on.

On the third day she came on a child sitting in the road.

He was perhaps six, and he had walked out of his boots somewhere behind him, and his feet were cut to ribbons by the hard ground. He was not crying. That was the worst part of it. He was sitting in the middle of a road in a burned country with his feet bleeding, and he was waiting, because someone had once told him that if he got lost he should wait.

Vasilisa sat down beside him, not too close.

"Those want washing," she said.

He looked at his feet as though he had not been introduced to them.

"I've got water," she said. "It's for the doll, but the doll can go without a day."

She washed the small feet with what she could spare, and while she worked she talked about nothing — the grain of the wood in a cart shaft, the way a hinge tells you it's about to go — because the words themselves did not matter, only that they kept coming, steady, the way a hand stays on a horse's neck. Somewhere in the middle of it the shaking started, which she had been waiting for. She let it run. She gave him the last of her crust and put the mended cloak over him and sang the second verse of the river song.

His people found them an hour later — a straggle of travellers who had lost him at a ford and had been going back along their own road shouting his name.

An older woman among them stood watching Vasilisa hand the boy over. She had the face of someone who had done a great deal of counting lately.

"Where did you learn to be that quiet with a frightened thing?"

"My father mended carts," Vasilisa said, which was not an answer, and was.

The woman looked at her a moment longer. Then the group went west and Vasilisa went east.

That night the deeper cold rose so strongly that it nearly put out the small warmth of the road. She stopped under a birch that still held a strip of white bark, fed the doll a crumb, and said the simple prayer her mother had taught her beside the old songs. She waited until the stillness inside her was quiet enough to walk on. It took most of the night.

---

The grove was where the old woman had said.

The birch stood scarred down one side, the bark gone black and curled, and living. Beneath it the spring came up clear and cold into a basin of stones that somebody, a long time ago, had troubled to set in a ring.

Vasilisa knelt. She fed the doll its crumb of bread and its drop of clean water. She crossed herself and said the quiet words.

Then she looked into the water, which was very still, and the water showed her what she had already been carrying: ease and lengthening. Nothing turned back. She had known it in the old woman's kitchen, and she had known it every step of four days, and she found that knowing a thing and being shown it are not the same, and that the second one costs more.

She let herself be angry for the length of about ten breaths. It was a clean anger and she did not do anything with it.

Then she filled the small vessel, stoppered it, and turned toward home.

---

The return was no easier than the going. But she walked it.

She gave her mother the Living Water in careful sips, the way you water a plant that has been let go too long. The pain loosened its grip. Colour came back a little into the face on the pillow. The breathing eased.

And more days were given — twenty-one of them, though nobody counted aloud.

They were good days. That is the part people do not expect. Her mother sat up and took an interest in the mending and criticised Vasilisa's stitching, which she had not had the strength to do in a year. She hummed. Once, in the late afternoon, she got as far as the second verse of a song about a birch that keeps secrets in its white skin, and for the length of it the house was almost what it had been before the hill burned.

The cold in the doll did not lift.

On the last evening the mother said, without any preamble, "Sit where I can see you."

Vasilisa sat.

"You've been carrying something since the night the thatch caught," her mother said. "Don't."

"Don't what."

"Don't do the face. I've had that face myself." She turned her hand over on the blanket, palm up, and Vasilisa put her own hand in it. "Did you think I couldn't feel you not saying it? Every night for a year, not saying it. It made a draught in the room."

"I didn't want you to have to hold it."

"I know. That's why I let you keep it." The grip tightened, briefly. "My mother's mother carried the same silence for her own mother, and she never spoke of it either, and I only found it out after. It's the last of the housework, girl. It gets done quietly or not at all. You did it right."

Vasilisa found she could not answer.

"Sing the river one," her mother said. "I don't want to be listening for anything."

So Vasilisa sang the song about the river that remembers every crossing, all the verses, in the low voice she had been taught it in, and somewhere in the fourth her mother's breathing changed and then, a while after, stopped, as quietly as a candle deciding.

Outside, on the scarred threshold, a white bird settled for a moment — pure as new snow against the burned wood — and lifted, and was gone. Vasilisa alone saw it.

She sat in the dark a long time. Then she got up, because there is a great deal to be done, and the doing of it is a mercy.

She crossed herself. She trimmed the lampada. She fed the little wooden doll its crumb of bread and its drop of clean water.

And the cold was gone.

Not warmth. Not comfort. Only wood — ordinary, smooth, and quiet in her palm, the way it had been before the night the thatch caught. The measure had been kept and now the measure was finished, and the doll had let it go, and Vasilisa stood in the middle of her mother's house holding a small plain thing that had nothing more to tell her.

That was when she wept.

And that is how Vasilisa walked the Road of the Living Water, and how the water eased what it could and gave twenty-one days, and how the knowing that had ridden with her since the burning of the High Place was set down at last on the night it came true.
